Japan's ningen dock: the full-day checkup

Japan's one-day ningen dock checkup is thorough and well established. What a standard day includes, resident and visitor prices, how to fit one into a Japan trip, and how to handle the language gap.

A ningen dock is a one-day, self-paid checkup that's been part of Japanese life for decades. A standard course at a Tokyo clinic is listed at about ¥73,000 including tax, and about ¥145,000 with a head MRI and MRA. Those are prices for people living in Japan; visitors are quoted separately. One university hospital's one-day program for foreign visitors, booked through a medical coordinator and including endoscopy and CT, costs about ¥400,000. Japan's strengths are high standards and safety. Its main hurdles for Americans are language, and a full report that can arrive weeks after you've flown home.

What the name means

"Ningen dock" means "human dry dock": like a ship, you come in for a full inspection.

What a standard day includes

Contents vary by center. A typical one-day course includes:

  • Physical exam and medical history
  • Blood and urine tests (lipids, glucose, liver and kidney function, thyroid, blood counts)
  • ECG
  • Lung function test
  • Chest X-ray
  • Abdominal ultrasound
  • Stool test for hidden blood
  • Eye and hearing tests
  • Bone density (often)
  • Stomach check by barium X-ray, or gastroscopy for an extra fee
  • Sex-specific exams

Some centers add tumor markers as standard; one university program for visitors includes more than ten. As with any package, ask which items fit your age and history (see our guide to over-screening).

Resident prices are not visitor prices. Tokyo Midtown Clinic says fees for visitors from abroad are different and quotes them separately. Juntendo's visitor program includes upper endoscopy and chest and abdominal CT, which the resident courses above don't, so the rows aren't like for like. Ask any center for its price for visitors, in writing and item by item, before you book. For US dollars, convert at the day's exchange rate.

If something needs treatment while you're there, ordinary hospital care is billed differently. Japanese medical fees are counted in points, normally worth ¥10 each. The national hospital in Shinjuku (JIHS, formerly NCGM) charges patients without Japanese health insurance ¥30 per point, three times the usual rate.

Fitting a ningen dock into a Japan trip

  • Set aside a morning, or a day. Tokyo Midtown Clinic lists about 2 to 3 hours for its standard course, 4 to 5 hours with the head MRI, and about 6 hours for its top course. At Juntendo the day ends between about 2 and 4 pm.
  • Fast beforehand, as the center tells you.
  • Mind the cancellation rules. Juntendo charges the full fee if you cancel within 14 days of the appointment, so book once your flights are fixed.
  • Expect part of the answer on the day and the rest later. Both clinics give some results the same day; Juntendo explains them after lunch. Juntendo's full report reaches visitors through the coordinator in about a month, usually after you're home. Ask for it by email, in English.
  • Put it early in the trip, so there's time for a follow-up test before you leave if one is needed.

The language gap

English support varies a lot. Some hospitals have international patient teams with interpreters: the Shinjuku national hospital has on-site English and Chinese interpreters on weekdays, plus tablet interpreting in 19 languages, though it warns they aren't always available. Others ask visitors to book through a medical coordinator. Before booking, ask:

  1. Is an interpreter included, or extra?
  2. Will the final report be in English? When, and how will it reach me?
  3. Will I get my scan files (DICOM)?
  4. Who explains results to me before I leave?

Getting there

Americans can visit for up to 90 days without a visa, and the US travel advisory for Japan is Level 1. Japan is also the closest of the Asian checkup destinations to the US West Coast.
