What if a checkup abroad finds something?

Any checkup can find something. How to plan for abnormal results before a checkup abroad: who explains them, what if it's urgent, leaving room in your trip, and getting your records home.

Plan for it before you book. Make sure a doctor will explain your results to you in a language you understand before you leave; find out what care is available if something urgent is found; get a written report in English and your scan files; and arrange with your own doctor at home to see you soon after you return. Schedule the checkup early in your trip, and don't make big decisions abroad in a rush.

It happens, and it's usually not serious

Any checkup can turn up something unexpected, especially one with lots of imaging. A review of 32 studies, covering about 27,600 apparently healthy adults who had MRI scans, found a possibly serious finding in about 4 in every 100 people having brain and body scans. Counting findings whose importance was unclear, it was about 13 in 100. Most findings aren't serious. But you need a plan for the few that are, and for the follow-up that even minor findings can need.

Before you book: ask the hospital

  1. Who explains the results to me, in English, before I leave? A written report alone isn't enough.
  2. If something urgent is found, what can you do here, and how fast?
  3. Will I get a written report in English, signed by a doctor? When?
  4. Will I get my scan files (DICOM), not just printed images?
  5. Can my doctor at home contact the doctor who examined me? Get a name and an email address.
  6. What does the price include if I need a follow-up test or visit? Some providers charge substantial extra fees for follow-up care.

Before you go: talk to your own doctor

  • Tell them you're planning a checkup abroad, and share the package list. The CDC suggests seeing your doctor, or a travel medicine clinic, well before the trip, ideally 4 to 6 weeks ahead. (Our guide has five questions to ask your doctor.)
  • Ask whether they'll review results and images from abroad, and in what format.
  • Agree on what you'd do if something is found.
  • Book a follow-up appointment in advance for shortly after you return.

Leave room in your trip

Put the checkup near the start of your trip, not the day before you fly home. Then there's time for the results talk, and for a follow-up test or a second visit if the doctor suggests one, without rushing a decision or changing flights.

Check your insurance

  • Make sure your travel medical insurance covers care abroad, and that you have medical evacuation cover. (See our guide to travel insurance vs evacuation cover.)
  • If you're on Medicare, it pays for care abroad only in rare cases. (See does Medicare cover you abroad?)
  • Find out what your US insurance will cover for follow-up tests at home after a finding abroad.

If something is found

SituationWhat usually makes sense
Urgent (needs care within days)Discuss options with the hospital doctor, your travel insurer's assistance line and your own doctor before deciding where to be treated
Not urgentTake the report and scan files home; see your own doctor as planned
UnclearAsk the hospital doctor how urgent it is, in writing, then talk to your own doctor

Whatever happens, don't feel pressured into treatment abroad on the spot unless it's a genuine emergency. Take time to talk to your own doctor. Before you leave, ask for complete copies of your records, in English. Our guide to records and follow-up after care abroad covers what to bring home.

Who interprets your results

The hospital doctor who examined you, and your own doctor at home. Not a coordinator or an agency, and not us: East&Well doesn't interpret results or give medical advice.
